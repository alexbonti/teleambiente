# Craft: making it actually funny

Read this before drafting. The mechanics are narrow on purpose — fake-news comedy gets
its power from looking like the boring real thing, then doing one absurd thing perfectly.

## The one rule

One absurd premise, played absolutely straight, escalated with real-looking detail. The
satire label is the only place the piece admits it is a joke.

## Headlines

Present tense. Subject, verb, object. Drop articles. No question marks. Capitalize every
word except short prepositions and conjunctions. The funniest headlines bury the joke in
a subordinate clause or a mundane qualifier.

Formulas that work:

- `Area <Profession> <Absurd Action> After <Real Trend>`
- `<Institution> Announces <Absurd Policy> To Address <Real Problem>`
- `Nation's <Group> Reportedly <Emotional Overreaction>`
- `Study Finds <Real Trend> Linked To <Absurd Consequence>`
- `Local <Person> <Overreaction> Over <Real Detail>`
- `Experts Warn <Trend> May Soon <Escalation>`
- `<Company> Introduces <Pricing Tier> For <Basic Human Experience>`

Calibration:

- Flat: `Airline Charges For Being Greeted`
- Better: `Airline Introduces $49 ‘Priority Existence' Tier, Includes Being Acknowledged By Gate Agent`
- Flat: `New Study Is About Office Attendance`
- Better: `Study Finds Employees More Productive When Nobody Knows Where They Are`

The better version is longer and more specific. Specificity is the joke.

## The lede

`CITY — A <specific person or role> <absurd action> <preposition> <real trend>,
<institution> said <day>.`

- Second sentence: one concrete number, price, or logistics detail.
- Third: the absurd thing is now policy, with a name and a tier structure.
- Do not summarize the headline. Move the story one step forward.

## The quote ladder

Three fictional voices, each one normalising the absurdity a bit more:

1. **The true believer** — the executive or official who explains the absurd thing as
   reasonable, in the language of the industry. Their justification should be airtight
   and appalling.
2. **The deadpan colleague or customer** — says a flat, literal sentence that makes the
   absurdity visible without commenting on it.
3. **The overcommitting expert** — adds authority to the nonsense: "we simply do not know
   the long-term effects, which is why we have doubled the budget."

Optional fourth: **the returning bit** — a small character from paragraph two comes back
with a new, worse detail.

Keep quotes to one sentence, and give each voice a different register: the true believer
runs long and jargon-thick (the best line in the piece is usually a thirty-word
justification of something indefensible), the deadpan colleague speaks in about six flat
words, the expert hedges in careful officialese. One length for all three flattens the
comedy. Attribute with a real-sounding title, not a joke title — the comedy is in what
they say, not in the label.

## Escalation

Every paragraph adds one of: a price tier, a policy name, a number, a new affected group,
a logistics problem, a pilot program, a waitlist, an internal memo. Never repeat the same
joke at the same altitude.

Escalation ladder for a pricing joke: fee for the thing → subscription for the thing →
tier where the thing is worse → bundle → waitlist → plan to expand to twelve cities.

## Kickers

The last line is a flat sentence that lands the joke sideways. Types:

- **The quiet future:** "The company plans to expand to twelve more airports by spring."
- **The deflation:** a character accepts the absurdity as normal.
- **The return:** the bit from paragraph two reappears, worse.
- **The literal detail:** a small, specific consequence nobody would think of.
- **The inverted outrage:** the only person who objects is objecting to the wrong part.

Never end on a sentence that explains the joke.

## Voice and rhythm

- Deadpan. No exclamation marks, no adverbs of funniness, no "hilariously", no
  "absurdly". The tone is a wire reporter who has seen everything.
- Short sentences next to one long, over-stuffed sentence. Vary the length.
- Use the real vocabulary of the field — jargon is free comedy because it is real.
- Concrete nouns: `a 2019 Subaru Outback`, `a spreadsheet named final_FINAL_v4`,
  `the third-floor break room`, `row 34B`.
- Two jokes per paragraph maximum. If a paragraph has no new information, cut it.

## Formats

| Format | Shape | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Straight news report | Banner, headline, dateline, inverted pyramid, kicker | Default; policy, business, tech, local |
| Wire release | PR-style headline, "For immediate release", boilerplate, fictional contact | Product launches, absurd corporate moves |
| Local paper | Small-town names, a school board, a diner, one indignant resident | Community-scale trends, wellness, schools |
| Explainer | "Why everyone is ...", numbered points, expert quotes | Discourse, internet culture, language |
| Op-ed | Fictional columnist, first person, one grievance, a turning point | Media, work culture, status anxiety |
| Science/health | Study, sample size, press office, cautious quote | Wellness and research trends — keep it non-actionable |
| Live blog / thread | Timestamped updates, each one worse | Fast-moving absurd events |

## Specificity drill

Before delivering, underline every noun phrase that could only belong to this story. If
you can swap in any other company or town and the sentence still works, replace it with
something real from the research or something absurdly exact.
